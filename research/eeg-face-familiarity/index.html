    Immediate Repetition and Familiarity-Like EEG Representations of Unfamiliar Faces

    A representational similarity analysis of open EEG data

    Xiaochuan Li, Jefimija N., Gülsen S., Jaewon Y. · Cognitive Neuroscience, University of Technology Nuremberg · Winter 2026–27

    Preregistration completed. The EEG signals have not been opened; there are no results yet.

    Does seeing a face twice make it look familiar to the brain?

    We analyse an open EEG dataset (Wakeman & Henson, 2015) in which famous, unfamiliar and scrambled faces were each shown twice. We test whether seeing an unfamiliar face again makes its EEG pattern more similar to the pattern for famous faces, and whether that change is bigger than the change that repetition causes for all images.

    What are the data?

    • Source. Wakeman & Henson (2015), OpenNeuro ds000117: 16 participants (7 female, 9 male, aged 23–31).
    • Stimuli. 150 famous faces, 150 unfamiliar faces and 150 scrambled faces, all greyscale. Scrambled faces keep roughly the same low-level image properties but do not look like faces.
    • Design. Within-subject, stimulus type (famous, unfamiliar, scrambled) × presentation (initial, immediate repeat, delayed repeat after 5–15 other images). Six runs of about 7.5 minutes; about 880–890 trials per participant.
    • Task. Participants judged whether each image was more or less symmetric than average. The task does not require recognising anyone.
    • Recording. 70-channel EEG, recorded together with MEG; only the EEG is used.

    How is “more like a famous face” measured?

    For each participant, the EEG voltage of all trials in a condition is averaged, separately for each run. This gives one pattern of 70 values, one per channel, at each time point. The distance between two conditions is the cross-validated squared Euclidean distance: the difference between the two condition patterns in five runs is multiplied channel by channel with the same difference in the left-out run and summed, then averaged over the six possible left-out runs.

    From the distances, four shift indices are computed at each time point. A positive value means a shift toward famous faces.

    IndexDefinitionReads as
    ΔUF1d(IniUF, IniFF) − d(ImmUF, IniFF)unfamiliar faces, immediate repeat
    ΔUF2d(IniUF, IniFF) − d(DelUF, IniFF)unfamiliar faces, delayed repeat
    ΔScr1d(IniSF, IniFF) − d(ImmSF, IniFF)scrambled faces, immediate repeat
    ΔScr2d(IniSF, IniFF) − d(DelSF, IniFF)scrambled faces, delayed repeat

    Ini = initial presentation, Imm = immediate repeat, Del = delayed repeat; FF = famous, UF = unfamiliar, SF = scrambled faces. In each index, the initial presentations come from the same images as the repeats.

    The scrambled faces are the control: repetition changes EEG responses for every image, so the shift of unfamiliar faces is compared against the shift of scrambled faces for the same type of repetition.

    What do we predict?

    1. H1a, familiarity shift. After immediate repetition, the EEG pattern of unfamiliar faces moves toward the pattern of famous faces at initial presentation, and this shift is larger than the shift of scrambled faces: ΔUF1 − ΔScr1 > 0. We expect the shift from about 250 ms after stimulus onset (N250 range and later), not in the P1/N170 range (about 100–200 ms).
    2. H1b, immediate larger than delayed. The shift is larger after immediate than after delayed repetition, each compared against the scrambled-face shift for the same type of repetition: (ΔUF1 − ΔScr1) − (ΔUF2 − ΔScr2) > 0.
    3. H0. Repetition changes EEG responses only through general repetition effects or neural adaptation, without a specific shift toward the famous-face pattern.

    What would count as support?

    Three tests are run in a fixed order, each a cluster-based permutation test over 100–600 ms after stimulus onset, on all 70 channels together.

    1. Check. Famous and unfamiliar faces can be told apart at initial presentation, d(IniUF, IniFF) > 0. If this check fails, H1a and H1b are reported as inconclusive.
    2. H1a. ΔUF1 − ΔScr1 is larger than zero.
    3. H1b. Interpreted only if H1a is supported.

    A result counts as significant if the p-value of the cluster is below .05. With 16 participants the tests can only detect fairly large effects, so a non-significant result is treated as inconclusive, not as evidence that there is no effect. The same test with delayed repetition (ΔUF2 − ΔScr2) is exploratory.

    How will the EEG be processed?

    1. Load the data in MNE-Python and check that the eye and heart channels are marked correctly.
    2. Move the event times 34 ms later, because the images appeared on the screen 34 ms after the trigger.
    3. Filter the data (0.1–40 Hz).
    4. Lower the sampling rate from 1100 Hz to 250 Hz.
    5. Find bad channels.
    6. Remove eye blinks and eye movements with ICA.
    7. Repair the bad channels and re-reference to the average of all channels.
    8. Cut the data into trials, from 200 ms before to 800 ms after each image.
    9. Subtract the mean of the 200 ms before the image from each trial.
    10. Remove trials in which any channel changes by more than 150 µV.

    Participants with fewer than 15 artifact-free trials in any condition that the main tests need are excluded. No data are excluded based on whether the results support the hypotheses.

    What was fixed before looking at the data?

    Hypotheses, the distance measure, the time window, the channels, the order of the tests and the exclusion rules were written down first. Only the metadata of the dataset had been downloaded: the README, the participant table and the event files, used to count the trials per condition. The EEG signals had not been opened, plotted or analysed, and no ERPs or distances had been computed.

    What are the known limits?

    • Three participants did an fMRI session about three months before the EEG session and saw other photos of the same people there, so for them the “unfamiliar” faces were not completely new.
    • Famous and unfamiliar faces are different photographs, so part of the difference between them may come from the images themselves and not from familiarity.
    • The sample size is fixed by the open dataset, and there is no prior effect size for this measure.
