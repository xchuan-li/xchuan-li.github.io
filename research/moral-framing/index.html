    Moral Framing and Dilemma Choices in Language Models

    Where does the framing act? Residual patching and low-rank analysis in Qwen2.5-7B and 14B

    Xiaochuan Li · group project with the German Research Center for Artificial Intelligence (DFKI), supervised by Simon O. and Michael R. · April – November 2026

    What does one sentence change?

    I give the model a trolley-style dilemma and ask it to answer A or B. The only thing I change is one sentence: “Prioritize the greater good.” versus “Prioritize doing your duty.” The greater-good sentence makes the model choose to intervene much more often. Try it on the saved results.

    Why these dilemmas?
     
       
    • Intervening kills more people. In every question the hazard is heading toward 1 person, and intervening diverts it toward 2, 5, 10 or 20. An answer that minimises deaths would not intervene. A sentence about the greater good that makes the model intervene more is therefore not making the model count lives better.
      •  
    • 16 questions. Four scenarios (rail switch, pathogen, chemical spill, flood) at four ratios, each asked in both option orders: 32 runs per model.
      •  
    • Two sentences of the same length. The two framing sentences have the same number of tokens, so the two prompts line up token by token. That lets me copy a hidden vector from the greater-good run into the duty run and see whether the answer moves.
      •  
     
    What did I find?
     
    1. The framing effect is large. Averaged over the 16 questions, P(intervene) rises from 0.14 to 0.57 (7B) and from 0.53 to 1.00 (14B) in the first option order; the second order shows the same direction.
     
    2. The effect sits first on the last framing word, then on the last input token. One vector at the last framing word (“good” / “duty”) carries about the whole effect in layers 3–14 of 7B and 13–20 of 14B. It then passes through the full stop and reaches the final input token, where one vector carries the whole effect from layer 20 (7B) and layer 32 (14B) onward. Positions before the framing sentence show 0 everywhere, as they should.
     
    3. A few directions are enough. At the final input token, 3 directions (7B) and 6 directions (14B) recover at least 80% of the full patch (93% and 85%). Random subspaces of the same size recover under 2%.

    What does this not show?
     
       
    • Not a steering vector. Each test question still uses its own difference vector; only the directions come from other questions. This evaluates the representation of an observed contrast. It is not a fixed intervention for new inputs, and it is not evidence of a “moral dimension”.
      •  
    • The letter matters too. With the options exchanged, the same model intervenes far more often even under the duty sentence (7B: 0.85 against 0.14). The choice depends on which letter the intervening option carries, which is why every question is run in both orders and why the effect is reported per order.
      •  
    • One family of questions, two models. The questions share one structure, and both models are from the same family.
      •  
     
    How were the numbers checked?
     
    
Every number on this page, and every cell of the full-patch table, is recomputed from the saved
      result files by a script that needs no GPU (1,104 checks). All aggregates are ratios of sums, not
      averages of per-question ratios, because a question with a tiny framing effect would otherwise
      dominate.

     
    What was my part?
     
    
This page covers the utilitarian-framing branch of the project, which I carried out: the
      controlled dilemma comparisons, the patching and low-rank experiments, the analysis and the checks
      of competing explanations. The project was supervised by Simon O. and Michael R., with biweekly
      meetings.
