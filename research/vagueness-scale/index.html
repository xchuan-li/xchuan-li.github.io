    The Direction of Disagreement

    What departures from the majority term teach a text-only learner

    Xiaochuan Li · University of Technology Nuremberg · Preprint, November 2026

    • PreprintLingBuzz
    • DOIZenodo
    • CodeGitHub

    Is disagreement noise, or evidence?

    Imagine asking a group of speakers to name a colour chip that lies between blue and green. Some will say blue and others green, but almost nobody will say red. Speakers of the same language do not draw category boundaries in exactly the same places, so a chip near a boundary receives more than one name.

    Disagreement of this kind is usually treated as noise. For a learner who has access only to names, the direction of disagreement records which categories are neighbours: departures from the majority happen mostly near boundaries and almost always use the term for the category on the other side.

    Why ask what a learner gets?

    Two reasons.

    1. The disagreement is routinely thrown away. Typological mode maps retain only each chip's majority term, and NLP pipelines combine several annotators' labels into one by majority vote.
    2. Some learners cannot observe the magnitude directly. A language model trained only on text never sees a chip, and neither does a congenitally blind child learning colour words. Nor can anyone directly observe the degrees described by probability words such as likely and possible. A learner with access only to names must infer how the categories are arranged from their use.

    The setting has two parties. A listener knows how each term is used on average and guesses where an object lies. A learner sees only sentences of the form <object> is <term> from unnamed speakers, and never the scale. Text-only language models are useful experimental learners for this problem: we control which naming sentences they receive while withholding the underlying scale.

    What did we find?

    1. A little disagreement costs the listener little and gives the learner evidence. When an object near a boundary receives the neighbouring category's term, the listener's error is small, because the object lies at nearly the same distance from the typical members of the two categories. For the learner, the same naming event is evidence that the two categories are adjacent. As the spread of boundaries decreases, the listener's extra error decreases quadratically, while the fraction of neighbouring pairs an idealized learner can identify decreases only linearly (Proposition 1).

    2. Without disagreement the order is not learned; with it, the order appears. In an artificial language with a known scale, small transformers trained only to predict the next word show no order in their object representations when all speakers share the same boundaries (at most one seed in ten reaches p < .05). When boundaries differ by about a third of a category width or more, all ten seeds encode the order, for five and for eight terms.

    3. In 92 languages, the direction of disagreement carries the colour geometry. In the World Color Survey, randomly replacing the minority terms, while preserving where and how often departures occur, reduces the recoverable colour geometry in all 92 languages for the naming text and in 90–92 languages for the trained models. Retaining only the majority term removes the geometry.

    How does disagreement put the order into the text?

    If all speakers shared one set of boundaries, every object would receive a single name. The text would tell the learner which objects belong to the same category, but not which categories are neighbours: any reordering of the categories would fit the text equally well. Once the boundaries differ slightly, an object near a boundary is named with both neighbouring terms, and those two names link the two categories.

    A classical method already reads the order off such text: spectral seriation of the co-naming table recovers the exact category order in all ten corpora once the spread is 5% of a category width. The experiments ask whether a learner that is only trained to predict the next word acquires that order.

    Does it depend on the learner?

    Other architectures. Four- and six-layer transformers, a two-layer LSTM and a pretrained model (Pythia-160M, fine-tuned) pass every criterion fixed in advance. With shared boundaries, at most 2 of 10 seeds reach p < .05; with a spread of 0.35 category widths or more, all seeds do. Real disagreement beats misdirected disagreement in 90–92 of 92 languages.

    English pretraining does not replace disagreement. When the terms are the pretrained model's own words tiny, small, medium, large, huge, assigned to the categories in their natural order, no seed shows order in the object representations as long as speakers agree; at least 8 of 10 do once they disagree.

    What the model does not do. Asked Which is bigger, the kourb or the gaind?, a question absent from training, the model picks the larger object in 48–51% of pairs in every condition, as it did before fine-tuning. The order is in the representations; the model does not use it for this question.

    How were the claims checked?

    1. Exploration on seeds that are not used later.
    2. Criteria fixed in advance. Claims, pass thresholds and analysis code were written down before the confirmatory runs.
    3. Confirmation on new seeds, judged only by the fixed script.
    4. Independent reimplementation. A second implementation was written from the written specification alone, without the reference code or results, and ran the same seeds. Effect sizes are reported as ranges across the two.

    What is still open?

    • The learners read naming sentences, not natural text. How this source of evidence combines with other cues in ordinary text is untested.
    • The representations are read where the learner predicts a term, so the training objective shapes the measured geometry; whether learners can use the recovered order in behaviour is open (the comparison question stays at chance).
    • Because speakers are unnamed, the predictions do not distinguish disagreement across speakers from inconsistency within a speaker; the colour data also do not separate boundary disagreement from perceptual error.
    • There are no data from human learners yet.

    Related work

    Why is language vague?

    Barton L. Lipman. Why is language vague? International Journal of Game Theory, 2025.
    Notes: Shows that when speaker and listener want the same outcome, vague usage cannot do better than sharp boundaries shared by all speakers. The result assumes a learner who can observe the magnitude a word describes; the present work asks about a learner who cannot.

    Steven Verheyen, James A. Hampton, Gert Storms. A probabilistic threshold model: Analyzing semantic categorization data with the Rasch model. Acta Psychologica, 2010.
    Notes: Threshold models of categorization place most disagreement on intermediate items. Each speaker's boundary in our setting is such a threshold.

    Order from agreement patterns

    Jonathan E. Atkins, Erik G. Boman, Bruce Hendrickson. A Spectral Algorithm for Seriation and the Consecutive Ones Problem. SIAM Journal on Computing, 1998.
    Notes: Seriation recovers a hidden order from which items share responses. Applied to the co-naming table, it returns the exact category order once boundaries differ; the experiments ask whether a next-word learner acquires that order.

    Colour naming

    Terry Regier, Paul Kay, Naveen Khetarpal. Color naming reflects optimal partitions of color space. PNAS, 2007.
    Notes: Typological mode maps keep each chip's majority term. The majority corpus in Experiment 2 measures what that step discards.

    Barend Beekhuizen, Suzanne Stevenson. Perceptual, conceptual, and frequency effects on error patterns in English color term acquisition. CogACLL, 2015.
    Notes: Perceptual geometry is recovered from colour naming most clearly when many languages are pooled. We ask what disagreement inside one language gives.

    Mostafa Abdou, Artur Kulmizev, Daniel Hershcovich, Stella Frank, Ellie Pavlick, Anders Søgaard. Can Language Models Encode Perceptual Structure Without Grounding? A Case Study in Color. CoNLL, 2021.
    Notes: Text-trained language models represent colour words in a geometry that partly matches perceptual colour space. Our learners see none of those contexts, only which terms speakers give to which objects.

    Disagreement among annotators

    Joshua C. Peterson, Ruairidh M. Battleday, Thomas L. Griffiths, Olga Russakovsky. Human Uncertainty Makes Classification More Robust. ICCV, 2019.
    Notes: Training on label distributions instead of majority labels improves robustness.

    Alexandra N. Uma, Tommaso Fornaciari, Dirk Hovy, Silviu Paun, Barbara Plank, Massimo Poesio. Learning from Disagreement: A Survey. JAIR, 2021.
    Notes: Surveys methods that treat annotator disagreement as signal. For graded labels, our results give a specific reason: majority voting removes the order encoded in the label distribution.
