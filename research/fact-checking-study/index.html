    Taking a Language Model’s Word for It?

    Does meeting a language model first change how people verify an answer?

    Designed in a group of three and run by the whole class; analysis, report and poster by Xiaochuan Li · University of Technology Nuremberg · Winter 2025–26

    • ReportPDF
    • PosterPDF

    Does the first source change how people verify?

    People increasingly hand parts of their thinking to external tools. One worry is that an answer from a tool treated as an authority is taken as a reason to believe, and is then checked against other sources less often. We asked whether meeting ChatGPT as the first available checking tool changes checking behaviour, compared with meeting Wikipedia, which is similarly familiar and encyclopaedic.

    The experiment is a first, small case of a larger question in my research program (2b): how does exposure to machine-generated language change people’s understanding and habits of thought? Here the exposure is one session, and the habit measured is whether people verify.

    What did participants do?

    1

    Answer ten general-knowledge questions with two options each, and rate certainty.

    What is the coloured portion of the eye called? Iris / Pupil

    2 · first check

    Wikipedia for one group

    ChatGPT for the other

    assigned at random; each answer could be checked or not

    3 · second check

    ChatGPT

    Wikipedia

    the other source, offered for every question

    4

    Rate how often each tool is correct, and how often you usually double-check.

    A check showed a static screenshot of that tool’s answer, so every participant saw the same content. The task description did not mention checking.

    What did we predict?

    1. H1. Participants who can check with ChatGPT first check more of their answers in the first round.
    2. H2. Participants who check with ChatGPT first check fewer answers with the other source in the second round. An answer from a tool treated as an authority is less often cross-checked.

    What did we find?

    Neither prediction was supported, and neither difference was reliable. With 47 participants in the analysis (29 Wikipedia first, 18 ChatGPT first), both differences ran against the predictions. The ChatGPT-first group checked slightly fewer answers in the first round, and more answers with the other source in the second.

    Wikipedia first (n = 29)ChatGPT first (n = 18)Difference
    First check6.24 (SD 2.80)5.78 (SD 3.64)−0.46; Welch t(29.4) = −0.46, p = .65, d = −0.15
    Second check, other source5.38 (SD 3.90)7.00 (SD 3.65)+1.62; t(38.1) = 1.44, p = .16, d = 0.43

    Second-check scores clustered at the extremes. Half of the ChatGPT-first group checked nine or more of the ten answers with Wikipedia afterwards; in the Wikipedia-first group many checked almost none with ChatGPT.

    What would the opposite pattern mean?

    The second difference is the larger one. If it held in a larger sample, it would suggest the opposite of the authority hypothesis: that an answer from ChatGPT prompts people to look elsewhere, and does not settle the question. With 18 and 29 participants and effect sizes of this magnitude, the study could not tell these patterns apart from chance, and we draw no conclusion about the direction.

    What does this not show?

    • One session. The study measures a single encounter with a language model’s answer. It says nothing about what long exposure does.
    • Small and unbalanced. The sample was recruited by convenience, largely among students, and the groups differ in size.
    • Checking was cheap. A check cost one click and showed a screenshot, which is much easier than using the real tools; the high rate of checking may reflect this.
    • The questions were easy for many participants, even after revision.
    • The second check was offered for every question, so it measures checking with a second source, not strictly double-checking the same answer.
    • No preregistration and no power analysis. The certainty and authority ratings were collected but are not analysed in the report.

    What should a follow-up change?

    • A larger, balanced sample.
    • Harder questions, calibrated so that participants are genuinely uncertain.
    • A checking step with a real cost, such as time.
    • A second round restricted to answers already checked, so that it measures double-checking.
    • An analysis that relates each check to the rated certainty of the answer and to the rated authority of the tool.

    How was the study run?

    The design came from a group of three and was adopted by the class; the whole class collected the data. Eight people took part in a pilot. The pilot showed that the online group assignment was uneven, which was fixed, and that the questions were too easy to prompt checking, so two were replaced with harder ones. The study was implemented in PsychoPy and run online through Pavlovia. I did the analysis and wrote the report and the poster.
