Abstract

This course project examines how readers interpret temporarily ambiguous sentence structures in Chinese and English. It involves constructing garden-path sentences, proposing accounts of the cognitive processes that unfold during reading, and identifying methods that could distinguish those accounts. The report presents illustrative materials in which a noun initially appears to fill one grammatical role but must later be assigned another. Initial structural commitment is considered separately from the completeness of subsequent revision. A proposed design combines early structural cues, regional reading measures and comprehension questions. The project was developed as a theoretical analysis and experimental design; no experiment was administered.

Keywords: garden-path sentences; incremental parsing; syntactic ambiguity; reanalysis; Chinese and English

1 Research question

Readers encounter a sentence incrementally. A prefix may support an interpretation that later words rule out. Garden-path constructions make this problem visible by placing initially plausible grammatical relations in conflict with the structure required by the complete sentence.

The project asks when readers commit to a structure, how additional information changes that commitment, and whether the initial interpretation can continue to influence understanding after revision. Chinese and English provide different ways to examine these questions because the order and form of their structural cues differ.

2 Sentence materials and structural analysis

The following example materials illustrate the proposed ambiguity types and early-cue contrasts. They are design examples; the strength and location of any garden-path effect would be established in a material pretest.

2.1 Chinese: apparent main-clause object versus relative-clause subject

(1a) 校长表扬了老师推荐的学生。
(1b) 校长表扬了由老师推荐的学生。

Intended interpretation: “The principal praised the students recommended by the teacher.”

At the prefix 校长表扬了老师 (“the principal praised the teacher”), a reader may initially assign 老师 (“teacher”) the role of object of 表扬 (“praise”). The continuation 推荐的学生 requires a structure in which the teacher is the recommender, while the students are the object of praise:

Initial candidate: [校长] [表扬了] [老师] …
Complete structure: [校长] [表扬了] [[老师 推荐 的] 学生]

The second verb 推荐 (“recommend”) supplies a potential restructuring cue, followed by 的 and the head noun 学生 (“students”). Chen, Grove and Hale (2012) discuss a related main-clause illusion in Chinese object-modifying relative clauses. The early 由 (“by”) in (1b) may help signal the modifying phrase before the noun is assigned a main-clause object role.

2.2 English: subordinate-clause object versus main-clause subject

(2a) While the teacher taught the students waited outside.
(2b) While the teacher taught, the students waited outside.

In (2a), the prefix While the teacher taught the students allows the students to be attached as the object of taught. On encountering waited, that noun phrase must instead be treated as the subject of the main clause:

Initial candidate: [While [the teacher] [taught [the students]]] …
Complete structure: [While [the teacher] [taught]] [[the students] [waited outside]]

The comma in (2b) supplies a clause boundary before the students. This contrast belongs to the NP/zero-complement ambiguity family used in garden-path research (Christianson et al., 2001). The final sentence says that the students waited; it does not specify whom the teacher taught.

3 Processing hypotheses

3.1 Initial structural commitment

One account proposes that the reader initially selects a preferred structure and revises it when a continuation becomes incompatible. Another proposes that more than one structure remains available, with their relative weights changing as words and contextual cues arrive. Both accounts can predict difficulty at a disambiguating region. A slowdown alone therefore does not distinguish them.

Early cues and preceding context provide a way to examine when alternatives become influential. Prefix completions can establish which continuations readers favour before disambiguation. Their distributions constrain the assumptions made about the initial structural preference.

3.2 Completeness of revision

A separate question concerns the outcome of revision. Readers may fully replace the initial interpretation, or retain some of its event relations while constructing a grammatical analysis of the complete sentence. Initial commitment and revision completeness are two dimensions, rather than mutually exclusive theories. Christianson et al. (2001) provide evidence that initially assigned thematic roles can persist in final interpretations.

4 Proposed experimental design

4.1 Conditions and material checks

The primary contrast is between a temporarily ambiguous sentence and a version with an earlier structural cue. The Chinese 由 manipulation and the English comma manipulation illustrate that contrast. A full item set would be checked for grammaticality, naturalness, initial interpretation bias and the position of disambiguation.

The Chinese cue adds a character and changes the construction, so its raw reading time cannot be treated as a perfectly matched control. Analysis would require careful region alignment and controls for length and lexical properties. Preceding contexts favouring different structures could provide an additional manipulation while holding the target sentence fixed.

For (1a), comprehension questions could ask Who did the principal praise? (teacher/students) and Who recommended the students? (teacher/principal). For (2a), a question about who waited checks the main-clause subject. A separate question can ask whether the sentence explicitly identifies the students as the teacher’s teaching object; this distinguishes the asserted relation from a plausible real-world inference.

Self-paced reading offers a practical measure of regional difficulty. Eye tracking adds information about regressions and rereading. Neither measure alone establishes the success of reanalysis, which is why comprehension questions are included.

4.3 Cross-language comparison

The examples compare a shared processing problem across different constructions. They are not word-for-word equivalents. A cross-language study would need to specify whether participants are native readers or second-language learners, account for proficiency and reading experience, and align conditions by their structural function rather than raw word position.

5 Predictions and interpretation

If a reader has strongly favoured the initial attachment, the ambiguous condition may show additional difficulty around 推荐／的／学生 in Chinese and waited and its following region in English. Earlier cues may reduce this difficulty. These are predictions about candidate materials, not measured effects.

If initial event relations persist after revision, final role questions may reveal responses consistent with the earlier interpretation even when the reader has continued through the sentence. Conversely, accurate answers alongside a slowdown would be compatible with successful revision. A combined pattern of prefix preferences, cue sensitivity, reading difficulty and comprehension is more informative than any single measure.

6 Conclusion

The project connects sentence construction with hypotheses about incremental understanding and with methods for evaluating those hypotheses. Its output is an experimental design: it specifies the structural ambiguity, candidate processing accounts, proposed contrasts and complementary measures. No participants were tested and no experimental results are reported.
