# Is the logic of natural language the logic of the logicians?

<p class="text-[var(--color-text-muted)] italic !text-lg !mb-8">How formal semantics turns intuitions about meaning into competing, testable hypotheses.</p>

The answer is not "no." Modern linguistics does not say natural language is illogical. Classical extensional logic gives us indispensable tools for representing entailment; what it does not settle on its own is what an ordinary modal sentence means, because those expressions draw on further parameters supplied by context, evidence, and conversational goals. Formal semantics is the study of that gap. Here is the arc in five steps, and where I come in.

## 1. One fixed form

Classical logic assigns a proposition a truth value and a form to match. "All ravens are black" is ∀x(Raven(x) → Black(x)); a single non-black raven refutes it. The system is clean and powerful for entailment: every proposition is true or false, with nothing in between. Necessity and possibility, in the basic modal reading, are treated the same flat way — *necessary* is true in all cases, *possible* in some.

## 2. Then natural language misbehaves

Now take an ordinary modal sentence: "It must be raining." The flat modal reading — necessity as "in all worlds it is raining," possibility as "in some" — does not fit. The speaker is not claiming it rains in every possible world; they are reasoning from the evidence they have (wet umbrellas, a darkened sky) to what those clues force. And strangely, "It must be raining" is *weaker* than a plain "It is raining": you reach for *must* precisely when you are inferring rather than seeing. A logic that makes necessity the strongest possible claim gets the direction backwards.

So the real question is not whether "It must be raining" is true. It is: what is the logical form of *must* in the first place? Traditional logic assumed the form was already known. Linguistics answered no: the logical form is itself the object of study. That reversal is one of formal semantics' deepest moves.

## 3. Not less logic, but more

Formal semantics answers with more logic, not less. Kratzer's account gives *must* a modal base (which worlds count as live, given the evidence) and an ordering source (how those worlds rank against a norm or a stereotype), so that *must* says the prevailing case holds in the best-ranked worlds still open. Flat necessity and possibility — □ and ◇ over all or some worlds — fall out as a special case. And there is more than one such logic: Lassiter reads modal force instead as a single degree of certainty on a scale, not a quantification over worlds at all. Both accounts are precise, and they disagree — which is what makes the question worth putting to evidence.

Linguistics never abandoned logic. It kept building new logic: modal bases and ordering sources, degrees, conditionals, presupposition, alternatives.

These traditions do not form a single inheritance line — the history is messier than that. But they supply different parts of the present question. Modal logic (Leibniz's quantification over worlds, Kripke's frames and accessibility relations) provides the formal structures. Linguistic semantics — Kratzer, and the degree camp after her — studies how those structures are realized in context. And cognitive experiments, like Leahy and Carey's, ask what observable behaviour would distinguish one representation from another. My undergraduate thesis was on Leibniz's *characteristica universalis*; the distance from there to here is real, though not a straight line.

## 4. Does the mind hold it?

Grant that *must* is best written as a modal base plus an ordering source. A new question opens at once: does a human mind actually represent that structure, or only a feeling of confidence attached to a guess? Reading which structure a mind holds, from behaviour alone, without seeing the mechanism, is the craft of cognitive and developmental psychology — and Leahy and Carey turned exactly this into an experiment, asking when a child comes to treat the merely-possible as genuinely different from the actual.

What that literature has largely left separate is the language itself. Nonverbal modal reasoning has been studied, and the acquisition of modal vocabulary has been studied, but the question of what the modal *expression* does to a child's subsequent reasoning sits between them.

## 5. What evidence could distinguish the accounts?

Now a version of the question that did not exist thirty years ago. The accounts disagree about what *must* contributes:

- worlds and an ordering: a modal base ranked by the evidence (Kratzer);
- a degree: a single point of certainty on a scale (Lassiter);
- a frequency: *must* simply tends to precede hedged claims;
- or nothing structured at all: *must* and its neighbours merely co-occur.

It is tempting to think a language model could settle this — decode its representation, name the format, read the answer off. I do not think the internal geometry adjudicates it: a direction that correlates with certainty is a direction that correlates with certainty, and calling it a set of worlds is a decision made outside the data. The accounts are better separated by what they *predict*. Do they differ in the judgments speakers accept, the inferences a sentence licenses, how a claim is revised when new evidence arrives, or how the pattern generalizes to sentences no one has seen? A language model is useful here for one reason: you can hold the input fixed, change a single thing, and check whether the predicted difference appears. That is worth exactly what the behaviour it explains is worth, which is why the behaviour comes first.

## An older project, inverted

There is a much older version of this. Leibniz wanted a *characteristica universalis*, a formal language able to express every thought. Frege, Russell, Tarski, and Montague carried the project forward, and their question was always the same: *can natural language be translated into logic?* My question inverts theirs. They designed the logic by hand. A model now learns its own, from language alone. So the question becomes: what did it learn, and does linguistic input contain enough to learn it from?

## What the evidence could decide

It would be a mistake to phrase this as "did the system learn the *correct* logic that formal semantics proposed." That smuggles in a strong and false assumption: that formal semantics has already delivered the one correct analysis. It has not. Modality alone has several live theories: Kratzer's modal base and ordering source, Lassiter's degree semantics, and further work on evidential and priority modals. Kratzer's is the most influential, not the only one.

So the framing I hold is this. Formal semantics offers a set of explicit, competing hypotheses about what modal expressions mean and do. My interest is not to crown one theory correct. It is to work out what each hypothesis predicts about the reasoning that follows an utterance, and to find evidence that can tell those predictions apart — evidence of a kind judgment data alone does not give.

Modality is where I work now, though not the limit of the question. It runs on to attitudes, evidentials, conditionals, and counterfactuals: each a place where the logic of natural language looks unlike the logic of the logic books, and a chance to ask what a linguistic expression does to the reasoning around it.
