# What Leibniz taught me to ask about language

<p class="text-[var(--color-text-muted)] italic !text-lg !mb-8">A personal reflection on my undergraduate project — background, not a research result.</p>

My undergraduate thesis was on Leibniz, and specifically on the *characteristica universalis*: his lifelong plan for a universal symbolic language in which every concept would be a calculable expression and every inference a calculation, so that two philosophers in disagreement could stop arguing and instead say *calculemus* — let us calculate. The thesis followed that ambition through his combinatorial, arithmetic, and geometric attempts and into the early formal logic that grew from them. At the time I did not think of it as having anything to do with machine learning. It was about Leibniz, and then it was finished.

What I notice now is that one question from it never left, and it is a question about language.

Leibniz's bet was that you could build an *alphabet of human thought*: a small set of primitive concepts, symbols to name them, and a calculus to combine them, so that the form of an expression would mirror the structure of what it meant. The dream failed — almost no one now thinks the strong version is reachable — but the interesting part was never whether it succeeded. It was the assumption underneath: that form can be made to *show* structure. When can the shape of a symbol reveal the concept it stands for? When can the shape of a sentence reveal the reasoning it licenses? And when is a notation just a habit that carries no structure at all?

Two details from the thesis still strike me. Leibniz encoded concepts as numbers so that a concept's prime factors were its parts — the same trick, three centuries early, that Gödel would later use to make logic talk about itself. And Leibniz, hunting for a script whose form carried meaning directly, became fascinated with Chinese characters, asking whether a finite set of basic signs could generate the rest. He was reaching, in the 1680s, for a version of the question I now ask across languages: how much of meaning is carried by the form, and how much by everything around it.

I am not building a universal character. My work runs the other way. Leibniz, and Frege and Montague after him, designed a logic by hand and asked whether natural language could be translated into it. I start from natural language as it is — messy, context-dependent, built differently in every grammar — and ask how *it* organizes information: which possibilities a sentence keeps open, which it rules out, what it lets you go on to infer. And where Leibniz would have constructed the structure, the systems I study have to *learn* it, from language alone. The question shifts from "can we design a form that shows meaning" to "can a learner recover the structure of meaning from the forms it is given, and is there enough in those forms to recover it from."

So the inheritance is narrow. Not a program and not a method — a question. Leibniz taught me to ask whether the form of a language shows the structure of its meaning. I spend my time now on the natural-language version of that question, and on what would count as an answer.
